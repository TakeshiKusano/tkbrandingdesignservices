What comes next is designing the customer's "state."

Apple, Disney, Duolingo — the world's most advanced companies aren't designing screens. They're designing what state they take a customer from, and what state they take them to.

For a decade, companies have chased "easier to use" and "more comfortable" — and arrived at the end of that chase to find every company's app looking startlingly alike. Excellent UX and CX have stopped being a weapon for winning and become the bare minimum condition for survival. This book proposes State Design: a design philosophy built on a single question — not how a screen feels, but what state does this service take the customer from, and what state does it take them to. People are not buying products, using services, or consuming experiences. They are paying money and time to obtain a "changed self."

Case Study

The world's leading companies are already designing "states"

Ten companies. Ten industries. Every one of them reveals the same underlying structure: Before → Transition → After.

AppleAnxiety, complexity→Reassurance, simplicity

Cutting features can itself be an active strategy — a novice becomes a "creator" within the first few minutes.

DisneyThe everyday, reality→A dream, a family memory

The State Journey is designed across an entire timeline — from anticipation to the afterglow back home.

DuolingoNo motivation→A daily habit

The streak isn't a gimmick — it's proof of change the user can feel for themselves.

HeadspaceRestlessness, scattered thoughts→Calm, focus

Strips features away, rather than adding them, to carve the shortest route to calm.

TeslaAnxiety about switching→Ownership, fandom

The car itself keeps evolving after purchase — owners become evangelists, not just customers.

AirbnbThe alienation of a tourist→The feeling of having lived there

Not a tourist — a temporary resident. That single reframe built a category of its own.

MUJIInformation overload, decision fatigue→Reassurance, a simple life

A philosophy of subtraction, engineered to lower the visual and mental load of choosing.

Hoshino ResortsFatigue, being buried in the everyday→Recovery, refreshment

Even the check-in flow is calculated to settle a guest's heart rate before they reach the room.

SmartHRHassle, psychological burden→"Done," reassurance

Proof that State Design applies just as effectively to a B2B, back-office product.

MercariGuilt of throwing something away→Value, a sense of accomplishment

A "thank you" from a buyer is the touchpoint that lets a seller feel the change themselves.

1. Minimize through subtraction

Apple and MUJI operate in entirely different industries, yet both shorten the psychological distance to the After state by stripping information away.

2. Show proof of change

Duolingo's streak, Tesla's update notifications, Mercari's "thank you." A state only truly changes once the customer can confirm it for themselves.

3. One unbroken story

Disney and Hoshino Resorts design the journey end to end. One weak touchpoint anywhere along the way, and the whole impression collapses.

And one more thing every one of them shares: none of them start from their own product description. They start from the customer's own trouble, or sense of guilt — Apple from anxiety about complexity, Duolingo from a tendency to quit after three days, Mercari from the guilt of throwing something useful away.

Framework

Seven tools for putting State Design into practice

The State Design equivalent of the customer journey map and the persona — usable on day one, with nothing but paper and a shared vocabulary.

01State JourneyMap the journey of a stateLines up "states" instead of actions along a timeline — confused, understood, sticking with it — because state is what's real to the customer.
02State MapDraw the map of a stateA bird's-eye map of every state, branch, and reversal — including the churn risk that never shows up in a retention-rate number.
03State CanvasA one-page design toolBefore state, trigger, anxiety/resistance, transition design, touchpoint, after state, emotional arc, failure pattern — eight boxes, one page.
04State KPIMetrics for state transitionsState-arrival rate, state-transition time, state-retention rate, state-reversion rate — a deeper layer above action-based KPIs.
05State UXUI design for state transitionsFour principles: transparency, minimization, emotional synchronization, and presenting proof that the state has changed.
06State TouchpointReread every touchpointAds, storefronts, support, invoices — rearranged onto a single state-transition map, exposing the "valleys" between departments.
07State Transition ModelDesign as probabilityState, transition, probability, intervention — shifting design from a static experience to a dynamic probability you can actually move.
Chapter 4

How AI is changing State Design

AI's real power isn't building UI faster. It's reading each customer's current state and supporting the transition to the next one.

Personalization

Starts from today's state, not fixed attributes like age or gender — because the same person can be confident today and anxious tomorrow.

Emotion AI

Reads irritation or hesitation from vocal tone and word choice — already coaching call-center operators in real time.

Adaptive UI

The screen itself changes shape with the state, the way Duolingo varies question difficulty or iOS Focus changes what you see.

Human OS

A platform that tracks an individual's mind-body state consistently across services, not just within a single app.

State Navigation

AI suggests the next step, the way car navigation shows a route — but the choice to follow it always stays with the person.

AI Agents

Skip the intermediate state of "hassle" entirely, carrying a task straight through to "done" on the customer's behalf.

Real-Time Optimization

The journey is never "built once and done" — it keeps being observed and fine-tuned, customer by customer.

Small business, too

None of this belongs to large corporations alone. As these capabilities get cheap, State Design becomes shared infrastructure any business can use.

"The more precisely AI can read a customer's state, the more unavoidable one question becomes: the line between supporting a state and manipulating one."

Chapter 5

State Design changes organizations, too

Employees move through states just as customers do. Training that only imparts knowledge stops one stage too early.

Knowledge → State → Behavior → Habit → Culture
Onboarding

Outsider → Teammate

Tension and alienation on day one become belonging — not through a thicker manual, but through one small, early success.

Leadership Development

Staff Member → Decision-Maker

The state of "being a leader" changes through the experience of deciding, not through more frameworks and theory.

Sales Training

Before a Deal → After a Deal

What separates top performers isn't the talk track — it's how calmly they enter, and how fast they recover from a loss.

The manager's role shifts from "administrator" to state designer — reading what state a direct report is in, and what state they need to be guided toward next.

Chapter 6 — 2035

The future of service design

UI recedes, and the state itself becomes visible — in voice, on the body, and in space.

UI Will Disappear

The act of "operating a screen" stops being the center of using a service, as AI agents and adaptive interfaces take over.

Voice AI

Vocal tone and word choice carry a richness of state that text and taps never could — the next interface is spoken.

Wearables & Biometrics

States that once had to be "reported" — fatigue, tension — become states that can simply be observed.

Spatial Computing

Lighting, sound, and scent adjust in real time to a person's state — Disney-level staging, democratized for any business.

State OS

Education, medicine, labor, and consumption connect around one question: what state is this person in, and what do they need?

The Challenges

Privacy, autonomy, and the EU AI Act's limits on emotion inference — the ethics must move at the same speed as the technology.

"Material civilization was built around products. Information civilization was built around networks. What comes next reorganizes social systems around the human state — a 'state civilization.'"
